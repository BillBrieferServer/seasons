If a friend described her life to you exactly as you're living yours, you'd know within about ninety seconds. You'd tell her she needs help, and you'd be right, and you'd be a little alarmed that she couldn't see it.

You can't see it in yourself for a specific reason: the entire job is pointing your attention outward. You've spent months or years monitoring somebody else's appetite, mood, sleep and medications. Nobody is doing that for you, and you've gotten out of the habit of doing it for yourself.

Burnout also arrives slowly enough that there's never a day it starts. Each week is only slightly harder than the last, and slightly harder is always survivable, right up until it isn't.

## What it actually looks like

Not a quiz. Just things families describe, usually about six months after they started happening.

**Resentment that catches you off guard.** A flash of anger at your mother for something small, asking the same question again, needing something at a bad moment. Then immediate guilt, because what kind of person feels that.

**Irritability you don't recognize.** Snapping at your spouse. Short with your kids. Feeling like a worse version of yourself in every relationship that isn't this one.

**Sleep that doesn't help.** Either you can't get it, or you get it and wake up just as depleted.

**Your own appointments keep sliding.** The dentist, the physical, the thing you meant to get looked at. Six months becomes eighteen. This one is nearly universal and it's a reliable indicator.

**Saying no by default.** Invitations feel like burdens rather than opportunities. Easier to decline, and eventually people stop asking.

**Going through the motions.** Doing everything correctly and feeling nothing while you do it. Efficient, competent, and hollowed out.

**Escape thoughts.** Imagining a version of your life where this isn't happening. Then a wave of shame about having imagined it.

**Drinking a little more than you used to.** Or eating differently, or scrolling until 2 a.m. The small anesthetics that accumulate quietly.

**Believing nobody else can do it right.** This one masquerades as devotion. Often it's exhaustion with a story attached.

If several of those landed, that's worth taking seriously. Not as a verdict about your character, but as information about your circumstances.

## The thing nobody says about resentment

Resentment does not mean you don't love them.

I want to be emphatic about this, because it's the belief that keeps people from asking for help. Feeling angry at someone you're caring for feels like evidence of a moral failure, so it gets hidden, and hiding it means never saying out loud that this is too much.

Resentment is what happens when a person carries a load past their capacity for a long time. It's a signal about the load, not about the love. Almost everyone doing this work has felt it. The ones who tell you otherwise are being polite.

## Why "take care of yourself" doesn't work

The standard advice is to practice self-care. Take a walk. Have a bath. Make time for yourself.

The reason this lands so uselessly is that it treats a structural problem as a mindset problem. You cannot rest if there is nobody to take over. A bath doesn't help when you're listening for the door the entire time. The obstacle isn't that you've forgotten to relax. It's that there is no coverage, and rest without coverage isn't rest.

So the real answer is unglamorous: **you need someone else to actually be there, on a schedule, whether or not you feel you've earned it.**

## What helps

**Respite, scheduled and recurring.** Not "when I really need it," because that day never gets identified. There's always a reason it's a bad week. A standing block, on the calendar, that happens regardless. Three hours every Wednesday used consistently does more than a heroic weekend once a year.

The Area Agency on Aging administers respite services and can tell you what you may qualify for. There can be waiting periods, so ask early rather than at the point of crisis. [Contact details are in the local services directory](/resources/senior-services-pocatello-bannock-county).

**Ask specifically.** "Let me know if you need anything" produces nothing, because it puts the work of identifying and requesting onto the person with no capacity left. Ask for exact things instead: *Can you take Dad to his Thursday appointment?* *Can you handle the insurance calls this month?* Specific requests get said yes to at a much higher rate than general ones.

**Let other people do it imperfectly.** Your brother will fold the laundry wrong. Someone else will make lunch differently. If you require that everything be done to your standard, you've guaranteed you'll do all of it, and the standard is not what's keeping your mother well. Presence is.

**Lower the bar on purpose.** Not everything has to be homemade. The house doesn't have to be what it was. Sustainable and slightly worse beats excellent and finite.

**Talk to someone who's in it.** Caregiver support groups exist for a reason, and the specific relief of hearing another person describe your exact week is difficult to get anywhere else. The Area Agency on Aging can point you toward what's available locally.

## When it's more than tiredness

Some of this stops being burnout and becomes something a doctor should hear about. Worth an appointment if you're experiencing:

- Persistent low mood that doesn't lift when circumstances briefly improve
- Loss of interest in things you used to care about
- Changes in appetite or weight
- Trouble concentrating on ordinary tasks
- Feeling hopeless about the situation or about yourself
- Drinking more than you're comfortable with

Depression is common among family caregivers and it is treatable. Being exhausted by hard circumstances and being depressed can look similar from the inside, which is exactly why it's worth having someone else look.

**If you're having thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out now. The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline is available by call or text, 24 hours a day.** You can also call it simply to talk when things feel like too much. You don't have to be in crisis to use it.

## The part I mean most

There's a belief running underneath a lot of this: that doing it alone is what love looks like, and accepting help is a form of giving up on someone.

It isn't. Your parent doesn't benefit from a version of you that's depleted, short-tempered and running on nothing. And if you break down entirely, which people do, the care stops completely, which is the outcome you've been working the hardest to prevent.

Getting help isn't stepping back from caring for them. It's what makes it possible to keep doing it.
