Most people expect there to be a moment.

A fall, a phone call from a hospital, something that makes the answer obvious. And sometimes that's how it happens. But far more often, families describe the same thing: nothing dramatic ever occurred. They just kept noticing small things until the small things added up, and then they spent a year unsure whether they were overreacting.

If that's where you are, this is for you. Not a quiz, and not a list of everything that can go wrong with a person after seventy. Just what I'd actually look at, and why.

## The one rule that matters most

Compare your parent to *themselves*, not to some general idea of how an older person should be doing.

If your dad has been a cheerful slob since 1974, a cluttered garage tells you nothing. If your mother has ironed her sheets every week of her adult life and the laundry is piling up in the hallway, that tells you a great deal, and it would look like nothing at all to a stranger.

You have information a professional walking in the front door doesn't have: decades of knowing what normal looks like for this specific person. The signal you're looking for is **change from their baseline**, not deviation from anyone else's.

## Start in the kitchen

I say this partly because of my background. I spent eight years as a health and wellness coach before this work, and food is where I learned to look first. But it's also just the most honest room in the house.

Open the refrigerator. You're not looking for whether it's full. You're looking for whether it's *moving*. Containers you recognize from your last visit. Milk well past its date. A freezer packed with meals somebody brought over months ago and nobody ate. Twelve identical jars of the same condiment, which usually means someone is shopping without a list and buying what they see.

Then look at the stove and the pans. If someone who cooked their whole life has quietly stopped cooking, that's worth understanding. It can be about energy, or grief, or a burn that scared them, or food not tasting like it used to. It's rarely about not caring.

And look at your parent. Are their clothes fitting the way they did? Weight loss in an older adult is one of the things I'd never wave off, and it's one of the things that most often goes unnoticed by the people who see someone every day.

## Then look at the paper

Mail is a good indicator because it accumulates visibly and doesn't lie.

Unopened envelopes, especially anything from a bank or an insurer. Notices marked second or final. Bills paid twice, or checks written and never sent. A stack of solicitations from charities nobody's heard of, which sometimes means a name has ended up on the wrong kind of list.

Money trouble in a person who has never had money trouble is a serious signal. Not because they've stopped caring, but because managing bills takes more organizing than people realize, and it's often the first complicated task to slip.

## Look at the house the way they would

Not whether it meets your standard. Whether it meets *theirs*.

Burnt-out bulbs nobody replaced. A dead smoke detector chirping that's been chirping since your last visit. Dishes that have been in the sink long enough to matter, in a house that never had dishes in the sink. Laundry, dust, and clutter that have crossed a line the person living there would once have cared about.

The pattern to watch for is maintenance falling away while the essentials hold. That usually means someone is spending all their available energy on the basics, and there's nothing left over.

## Look at their week

Ask what they did last week, and listen for names.

Someone who used to go to church, to a card game, to lunch with friends, and now describes a week where nobody came by: that's not a scheduling problem. Isolation compounds. It affects appetite, sleep, mood, and how quickly someone bounces back from being sick. It's a health issue, not a social one, and it's often the first thing to slide because it's the easiest thing to cancel.

Also worth noticing: whether they've stopped driving somewhere they used to go. People frequently narrow their world quietly rather than announce that something has gotten hard.

## What belongs with a doctor, not with you

Some of what you might notice isn't in the "life is getting heavy" category at all, and I want to be clear about the difference, because the response is different.

Take to a doctor, promptly:

- Confusion that's new, or getting worse
- Unexplained weight loss
- New unsteadiness, or falls, including any fall they didn't tell you about
- Medications being missed, doubled, or muddled
- A noticeable change in personality or mood

These can have causes that are treatable, and some of them are surprisingly ordinary: a medication interaction, an infection, a thyroid issue, poor sleep. That's exactly why they belong with a physician rather than with a family member trying to figure it out from the outside. I'm not able to assess any of it, and neither is anyone else who isn't a clinician.

What I *can* help with is everything in the other category: the [errands, the meals, the driving, the house, the company](/services). The heaviness of daily life, which is where most families actually are.

## What to do with what you noticed

Here's the mistake I'd steer you away from: going home, making a list, and presenting it.

I understand the impulse. You've been worried, you've finally got evidence, and you want to be taken seriously. But a list of everything that's slipping, read aloud to the person it's about, lands as an indictment, and it usually produces exactly the response you didn't want.

Take the list, and pick one thing. The one that would take the most weight off. Then start there, with that specific task, rather than with a conversation about how they're doing overall.

If you've already tried and gotten a firm no, that's common, and it's its own subject. I've written separately about [what to do when an aging parent says no to help](/resources/aging-parent-refuses-help).

And if you're reading this from Boise, or Seattle, or anywhere that isn't down the street, know that noticing from a distance is harder and you're not failing at it. Visits are snapshots. Give yourself permission to trust what you saw in the snapshot.
